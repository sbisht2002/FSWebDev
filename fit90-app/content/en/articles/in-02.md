Nobody arrives knowing exactly what their partner enjoys. Everyone is different, preferences change over time, and what someone liked with a previous partner may not be what they like with you. The only reliable way to find out is to ask, listen and pay attention. Curiosity is far more attractive than guesswork.

## Why asking matters

Many people find it hard to say what they want, even to someone they love. They may worry about seeming demanding, embarrassing themselves or hurting their partner's feelings. When you make it easy and safe to share preferences, you remove that barrier, and intimacy usually becomes more relaxed and enjoyable for both of you. Research on couples links this kind of open sexual communication with greater satisfaction.

## Questions that open the door

Choose a relaxed moment, outside the bedroom, and keep the tone light and curious:

- "What's something you really enjoy when we're close?"
- "Is there anything you'd like more of? Or less of?"
- "Is there anything you've wondered about trying?"
- "How do you like to be touched when you're feeling stressed or tired?"
- "What makes you feel most relaxed with me?"

You can also share first, which makes it easier for your partner to open up: "I really love it when you..., and I'd love to know what you enjoy most."

## During intimacy

Simple check-ins work well:

- "Do you like this?"
- "Softer or firmer?"
- "Slower or faster?"
- "Would you show me?"

Notice your partner's responses: breathing, sounds, movement, relaxation. These signals are useful, but they're not a substitute for asking, especially early on or when trying something new.

## Making it safe to answer honestly

- **Thank them** for telling you, even if the answer surprises you.
- **Don't get defensive.** "I'd like more of this" isn't criticism of you. It's a gift of useful information.
- **Respect limits.** If your partner isn't interested in something, accept it without pushing or bringing it up repeatedly.
- **Be patient.** Some people need time and several small conversations before they feel comfortable sharing.

## Sharing your own preferences

Your preferences matter too. If performance worries have made you focus only on "doing it right", you may have lost touch with what you enjoy. Practices like sensate focus can help you rediscover this. When sharing, keep it positive and specific: "I love it when you..." works much better than "I don't like it when you...".

## A playful way to start

Some couples find it easier to talk through a light, low-pressure exercise. Each of you writes down three things you enjoy, two things you'd like more of, and one thing you're curious about but not sure of. Swap lists and talk about them over a cup of tea. There's no obligation to try anything on the list. It's simply a way to learn about each other and to practise talking openly.

## When you can't find the words

It's fine to say, "I'm not sure how to put this," or "This feels a bit awkward to talk about." Naming the awkwardness often makes it smaller, and it invites your partner to be gentle with you too. Showing, rather than telling, can also help, for example by guiding your partner's hand.

## Preferences change

Stress, health, tiredness, life stages and simply getting older can change what feels good. Keep the conversation going over the years rather than assuming answers from months ago still hold. Couples who keep talking tend to keep enjoying each other.

## Respect and comfort come first

Exploring new things should always be mutual, consensual and comfortable for both of you. Never pressure a partner into anything, and feel free to say no yourself. Feeling safe is the foundation of feeling good.
