"Spectating" is when part of your mind steps outside and watches: checking, judging, predicting. "Is it working? How long has it been? What are they thinking?" It's one of the main ways anxiety gets in the way of arousal and enjoyment. The antidote is learning to bring your attention back to **sensation**, again and again.

## Why attention matters

Arousal grows when your attention is on what you're feeling: touch, warmth, closeness, breath. It fades when attention moves to judgement or worry. Think of attention like a spotlight. You can't switch off anxious thoughts by force, but you can keep moving the spotlight back to your senses.

## Training attention outside the bedroom

Like any skill, this is easiest to learn when nothing is at stake. Try these for a few minutes a day:

**Five senses check.** Wherever you are, notice five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can feel, two you can smell and one you can taste. Each time your mind wanders, gently return to the next sense.

**Mindful shower.** Notice the temperature of the water, where it lands, the sound, the smell of soap. When thoughts about the day pull you away, come back to the water.

**Body scan.** Use one of the body-scan sessions in the audio library. You'll move attention slowly through your body, noticing sensations without needing to change them.

**Eating one thing slowly.** Take a piece of fruit and notice its texture, taste and smell for a whole minute.

The point isn't to empty your mind. It's the moment of noticing you've drifted and coming back. That moment is the exercise. Every return strengthens the skill.

## Bringing it into intimacy

During intimacy, the same steps apply:

1. **Notice** when you've started spectating. Often the clue is a "What if..." thought or checking yourself.
2. **Name it** silently: "Spectating."
3. **Breathe** one slow breath, letting your belly and pelvic floor soften.
4. **Return** to a specific sensation: the feel of your partner's skin under your hand, the warmth where your bodies touch, your breath.
5. **Repeat** as many times as needed, without judging yourself for drifting.

Focusing on giving pleasure, curiously noticing how your partner responds, also pulls attention out of self-monitoring. Sensate-focus exercises, covered in their own lesson, are built around exactly this shift.

## What the evidence says

Mindfulness-based approaches have good evidence for reducing anxiety in general. Studies of mindfulness for sexual difficulties are more developed for women than for men, and evidence specifically for men's performance anxiety is still limited. Even so, attention training is low-risk, it fits with how arousal works, and many sex therapists use it. It's worth practising, with realistic expectations.

## A two-minute practice for today

Sit comfortably and close your eyes. Breathe slowly. Bring your attention to the feeling of your hands resting on your legs: the weight, the warmth, the texture of the fabric. When a thought pulls you away, notice where it went, say "thinking" silently, and return to your hands. Then move your attention to your feet on the floor, then to your breath in your belly. Do this for two minutes. That's the whole exercise, and doing it daily builds the same muscle you'll use during intimacy.

## Curiosity instead of checking

Spectating asks "Am I doing well enough?" Sensation asks "What does this feel like right now?" The second question is curious rather than judging. When you notice yourself checking, try swapping in curiosity: what's warm, what's soft, what's pleasant, what does your partner seem to enjoy?

## Be patient

Your mind will wander, often within seconds. That's not failure. It's what minds do. Each time you notice and come back, you're doing the work. Most people find that after a few weeks of daily practice, coming back to sensation gets quicker and the anxious commentary gets quieter.
