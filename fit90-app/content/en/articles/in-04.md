Many men measure a sexual experience by how long intercourse lasted or whether they "performed". But when partners are asked what makes intimacy great, they rarely mention stamina. They talk about feeling desired, cared for, relaxed and connected, and about a partner who pays attention to what they enjoy.

## Intercourse isn't the only goal

Research shows a striking gap: in a large US study, heterosexual women reported reaching orgasm much less often than men during partnered sex. Women who reported more frequent orgasms were more likely to say their partner included a variety of touch, kissing, longer intimacy and communication about what they wanted. In other words, attentiveness and variety mattered more than intercourse alone.

This is good news if you're working on early ejaculation or erections. Your partner's enjoyment doesn't depend only on intercourse. Touch, closeness and attention can be just as important, and often more so.

## What attentiveness looks like

- **Noticing responses:** breathing, relaxation, sounds and movement tell you a lot.
- **Asking:** "Do you like this?" "Softer or firmer?" "Is this good?"
- **Following their lead:** if your partner guides your hand or changes the pace, go with it.
- **Being present:** attention on your partner rather than on your own performance. This also quietens the anxious "spectating" mind.
- **Taking your time:** there's no finish line.
- **Variety:** kissing, massage, touch of the whole body, and other forms of intimacy alongside or instead of intercourse.

## Pleasure isn't a performance either

Focusing on your partner doesn't mean putting on a show or pressuring them to respond in a particular way. Avoid asking repeatedly whether they're close to orgasm or treating their pleasure as a test of your skill. That just moves the performance pressure onto them. The aim is shared enjoyment, curiosity and care.

## When ejaculation happens early

If you finish sooner than you'd like, intimacy doesn't have to end. Stay close and affectionate, and continue to focus on your partner's pleasure in other ways if they want to. Many couples find this takes enormous pressure off, and partners often appreciate it greatly.

## When your erection fades

Erections naturally come and go, especially during longer intimacy or when you're anxious. Instead of stopping or apologising, keep touching, kissing and connecting. Shifting attention to your partner and to sensation often helps arousal return, and if it doesn't, there are still many ways to enjoy each other.

## Learning without assumptions

Every person is different. What one partner loved, another might not enjoy at all, and what someone enjoys can change from day to day. Treat each experience as a chance to learn, rather than assuming you already know. Ask, notice and adapt, and be patient with yourself as you learn. Attentiveness is a skill that grows with practice, and it's one of the most appreciated things a partner can offer.

## After intimacy

Attentiveness doesn't stop when intimacy ends. Staying close, cuddling, talking and checking in afterwards helps your partner feel cared for and builds closeness for next time. The lesson on aftercare explores this further.

## Don't forget yourself

Focusing on your partner doesn't mean ignoring your own needs. Good intimacy is mutual. Share what you enjoy too, and let yourself receive care and attention. Generosity on both sides builds trust and closeness over time.

## A conversation to have

Outside the bedroom, you might say: "I want to make sure you enjoy our time together as much as I do. What do you love most? Is there anything you'd like more of?" Then listen without judging. It may be one of the most valuable conversations you have.
