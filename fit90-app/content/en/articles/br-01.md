Breathing is one of the few body systems you can control directly, and it is wired into the systems you can't. When you're anxious, your breathing gets fast and shallow. When you're calm, it slows down. The useful part is that this works both ways: slowing your breathing on purpose can help your body shift towards calm.

## What's going on in your body

Your nervous system has two broad modes. One, often called "fight or flight", speeds up your heart, tenses muscles and sharpens alertness. The other, "rest and digest", slows things down and lets your body relax.

Arousal and erections depend partly on the relaxed mode. That's one reason anxiety and erection problems often go together: a body braced for danger isn't set up for pleasure. Lasting longer is also linked to staying calm rather than tense and rushed.

Slow breathing, especially with a longer breath out, encourages the relaxed mode. A 2018 systematic review found that slow breathing, around six breaths a minute, was linked with more relaxed nervous-system activity and with people feeling calmer and less anxious. The studies were mostly small, so the effect is real but modest. It is a tool, not a fix-all.

## The patterns in this app

**Slow breathing (in 4, out 6).** About six breaths a minute. A good everyday choice for settling down, before a session, or before bed.

**Extended exhale (in 4, out 8).** The longer breath out adds an extra calming nudge. Useful when you notice your heart racing.

**Box breathing (in 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4).** The steady rhythm gives your mind something to follow, which helps when thoughts are racing. Some people find the holds uncomfortable. If so, shorten them or switch patterns.

**Physiological sigh (two breaths in, one long breath out).** Breathe in through your nose, then take a second short "top-up" breath, then breathe out slowly through your mouth. A 2023 study found that five minutes a day of this pattern improved mood and lowered breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation in the same study. It was one study of healthy volunteers, but it's quick and easy to try.

## A simple daily habit

Pick one pattern and one time of day, such as right after brushing your teeth at night. Do three minutes. After a week, try it at a moment when you feel a little stressed, like before a meeting. The more familiar the rhythm becomes when you're calm, the easier it is to reach for when you're not.

## How to breathe well

- **Breathe low.** Let your belly rise as you breathe in, rather than lifting your shoulders and chest. One hand on your belly helps you feel it.
- **Use your nose** to breathe in when you can. It slows the breath down naturally.
- **Don't force it.** If you feel light-headed or uncomfortable, go back to normal breathing for a few breaths, then try a shorter pattern.
- **Little and often.** A few minutes daily builds the skill so it's there when you need it.

## Using breathing in everyday life

- Before a pelvic-floor session, to soften tension
- When you notice anxious thoughts building
- Before intimacy, as part of a calm-down routine
- During arousal, combined with relaxing your pelvic floor (see the last-longer lessons)
- At bedtime, to help you wind down

## A note of caution

Breathing exercises are safe for most people. If you have a lung or heart condition, keep breath-holds short and gentle, and ask your doctor if you're unsure. If breathing exercises make you feel panicky, stop and try a simpler pattern with no holds. If anxiety is affecting your daily life, please talk to a doctor or psychologist. Breathing helps, and it works even better alongside proper support.
