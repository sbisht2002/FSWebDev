Anxiety isn't only a feeling in your body. It's also driven by thoughts: quick, automatic predictions that flash through your mind, often so fast you hardly notice them. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) starts with a simple idea: if you can catch these thoughts, you can start to question them, and their grip loosens.

## Automatic thoughts

Automatic thoughts are the instant interpretations your mind makes. In sexual situations, anxious ones often sound like:

- "What if it happens again?"
- "I'm going to lose my erection."
- "She'll think I'm useless."
- "I have to last at least ten minutes."
- "Everyone else finds this easy."
- "If this goes wrong, the relationship is over."

They feel true in the moment. But thoughts are not facts. They're guesses, and anxious guesses are usually skewed towards the worst case.

## Common thinking traps

Psychologists have names for patterns that anxious minds fall into. You may recognise some:

- **Fortune telling:** predicting failure before anything has happened.
- **Mind reading:** assuming you know what your partner thinks ("They're disappointed").
- **All-or-nothing thinking:** it was either perfect or a disaster.
- **Catastrophising:** jumping to the worst possible outcome.
- **Overgeneralising:** "It happened once, so it'll always happen."
- **"Should" rules:** "A real man should always be ready." These rules often come from porn, jokes or friends, not from real life.
- **Discounting the positives:** ignoring the times things went well.

Naming the trap is powerful in itself. "Ah, that's fortune telling again" puts a little distance between you and the thought.

## How to catch them

Thoughts are easiest to notice through feelings. When you feel a jolt of anxiety, ask: "What was going through my mind just then?" Write down the thought word for word, even if it sounds silly.

Good moments to practise:

- before a date or time with a partner
- when you notice yourself avoiding intimacy
- after an experience that didn't go as you'd hoped

## Using your journal

The thought journal in this app is built for this. Each entry asks for:

1. **The situation:** what was happening.
2. **The anxious thought:** in your own words.
3. **How strong the feeling was,** from 0 to 10.
4. **A more balanced thought** (you'll learn how in the next lesson).
5. **How strong the feeling is now.**

Everything you write is encrypted and stays on your phone. You can delete entries any time.

## Spotting the rules underneath

After a few entries, look for patterns. Do the same themes keep coming up, such as fear of judgement, a belief that sex must last a certain time, or a sense that your worth depends on performance? These deeper rules are often where the real work is, and seeing them clearly is a big step.

## A worked example

Rahul, 26, noticed his heart racing on the drive to his girlfriend's flat. When he paused to ask "What's going through my mind?", the thought was: "Tonight I'll mess it up like last time, and she'll get bored of me." Writing it down, he could see two traps at once: fortune telling ("I'll mess it up") and mind reading ("she'll get bored"). He rated the anxiety at 8 out of 10. Just naming the traps brought it down a little, to about 6, before he'd even started answering the thought.

## Thoughts versus feelings

It helps to separate thoughts from feelings. "I feel anxious" is a feeling. "I'm going to fail" is a thought. Feelings are real and worth respecting. Thoughts are ideas that may or may not be accurate, and they can be examined.

## A gentle reminder

You're not trying to stop thoughts. Everyone has anxious thoughts. The aim is to notice them, recognise them as guesses rather than facts, and choose how much attention to give them. With practice, this becomes quicker and easier.
