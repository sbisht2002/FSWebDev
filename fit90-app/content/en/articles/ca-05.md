When something makes us anxious, the natural response is to avoid it. With performance anxiety, avoidance can look like turning down dates, staying up late so you don't have to go to bed at the same time as your partner, cutting intimacy short, or always needing a drink first. Avoidance brings quick relief, but it keeps the fear alive, because you never get the chance to learn that you can cope.

Gentle exposure is the opposite: approaching the things you fear, in small, manageable steps, until they feel less threatening. It's one of the best-supported ideas in anxiety treatment generally.

## Why avoidance backfires

Every time you avoid something, your brain files it under "dangerous, and I escaped". The next time, the alarm rings louder. Avoidance can also create distance in a relationship, and a partner may misread it as lack of interest, which adds strain on both sides.

Exposure works by giving your brain new information: "I stayed, I felt anxious, and the anxiety rose and then fell. Nothing terrible happened." Repeated, that new learning gradually turns the alarm down.

## Building your ladder

Make a list of situations linked to your anxiety, from mildly uncomfortable to very scary. Rate each from 0 to 10 for how anxious it makes you. Then arrange them like the rungs of a ladder. For example:

1. Reading about performance anxiety without skipping the hard parts (2/10)
2. Using a body-scan audio session focusing on the whole body (3/10)
3. Talking to your partner about wanting to take things slowly (5/10)
4. Kissing and cuddling with an agreement that it won't go further (4/10)
5. Sensate-focus touch with no goal of intercourse (5/10)
6. Being intimate without alcohol (6/10)
7. Continuing to be close even if your erection fades (7/10)
8. Intercourse with permission to stop at any time (8/10)

Your ladder will be different. That's the point: it's yours.

## How to climb

- **Start low,** with something around 3 or 4 out of 10.
- **Stay with it** long enough for the anxiety to start to come down, rather than escaping at the peak. Use slow breathing to help.
- **Repeat** a step several times until it feels noticeably easier (around 2 or 3 out of 10) before moving up.
- **Drop the safety behaviours** gradually: things like needing a drink, constantly checking your erection, or rushing to "get it over with".
- **Notice what actually happened** compared with what you predicted. Write it in your journal.

## Pacing and kindness

If a step feels too big, break it into smaller ones. If you have a hard day, go back a rung. Progress usually zigzags upwards rather than climbing smoothly, and that's completely normal.

If you have a partner, sharing your ladder, or at least the idea behind it, can make a huge difference. Many couples find that agreeing to take intercourse off the table for a few weeks (as in sensate focus) removes enormous pressure and makes the lower rungs enjoyable.

## Working with alcohol

Many men use alcohol to take the edge off nerves. In small amounts it can relax you; in larger amounts it makes erections harder and dulls sensation. It also stops you learning that you can cope without it. If drinking has become a safety behaviour, gradually reducing it is a worthwhile rung on your ladder.

## When to get more help

If your anxiety is very high (8 or more out of 10 for most steps), if it's affecting your work or friendships, or if you have panic attacks, please talk to a psychologist or doctor. Exposure is often done with a therapist's support, and that's a sign of good sense, not weakness. In India, Tele-MANAS (14416) offers free support any time.
