Practising on your own first is the right way to learn the techniques. It removes pressure while you build awareness and control. But the real goal is usually to feel more relaxed and in control with a partner. This lesson is about making that move gently, at your own pace.

## When are you ready?

There's no fixed timeline. A good sign is when, in solo practice, you can usually notice arousal at around 7, bring it down with a pause or breathing, and rate your sense of control at 6 or more out of 10 most of the time. If you aren't there yet, that's fine. Keep practising.

If you're single, you can still use everything in this program. These skills will be there when you need them.

## Talking about it first

Bringing this up can feel daunting. In practice, most partners respond with relief and kindness, because they may have noticed your worry and wondered whether it was about them. A calm, honest conversation outside the bedroom usually works best.

You might say something like:

> "I've been working on feeling more relaxed and in control during sex. There are some exercises I'd like to try with you. It might mean slowing down and pausing sometimes. Would you be up for trying it with me?"

Make it clear that this is something you're doing to enjoy time together more, not a test, and that your partner's comfort matters too. Ask what they would enjoy. Some couples find this conversation brings them closer.

## Taking the pressure off

Couples therapists often suggest agreeing that, for a few weeks, the aim is **not** intercourse or "lasting long". The aim is to enjoy touch, practise the techniques and communicate. Removing the goal removes much of the anxiety. The sensate-focus lessons describe a structured way to do this.

## Step by step

1. **Start with what you practised alone,** but with your partner present and involved: slow pace, regular check-ins on your arousal number, pauses when you reach 7.
2. **Use a simple signal** for pausing, such as a word or a touch on the hand, so you don't need to explain in the moment.
3. **Your partner can help** with stop-start or the squeeze technique, if you're both comfortable.
4. **Fill the pauses with closeness:** kissing, touching other parts of the body, talking, breathing together. A pause doesn't have to feel like a break.
5. **Progress gradually,** for example by trying different positions or less frequent pauses, only when the previous step feels comfortable.

## Positions and pace

Many men find they have more control when they are lying on their back and their partner sets the pace, because their own muscles are more relaxed. Slower, deeper breathing and slower movement both help. Experiment together and talk about what feels good for both of you.

## When things don't go to plan

Some sessions will end sooner than you hoped. That's completely normal. Stay warm and connected: there are many ways to continue giving and receiving pleasure, and many partners care more about closeness than about time. The lesson on handling an "off night" has more ideas.

## Keep logging

Log partnered practice the same way: just the technique, "with a partner", and your sense of control from 0 to 10. Over weeks, the trend usually tells a more encouraging story than any single night.

## If progress stalls

If after several weeks of practice you aren't seeing change, or early ejaculation has always been there and is distressing, please talk to a doctor. Medical treatment combined with these skills is often the most effective approach. A qualified sex therapist can also help you both.
