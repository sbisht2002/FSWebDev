If there's one skill that improves intimacy more than any technique, it's communication. Research on couples consistently finds that people who talk openly about sex, including what they like, what they don't and how they feel, tend to be more satisfied with both their sex lives and their relationships. And at the heart of good communication is consent.

## What consent means

Consent means everyone involved freely and clearly agrees to what's happening, every time. In practice:

- **It's freely given.** Nobody is pressured, guilted, threatened or too drunk to decide.
- **It's specific.** Agreeing to one thing doesn't mean agreeing to everything.
- **It's ongoing.** Anyone can change their mind at any point, even midway, and that's respected immediately.
- **It's enthusiastic.** Look for a clear "yes", in words or obvious, willing participation, not just the absence of a "no".
- **It can't be given** by someone who is asleep, very drunk, high, or under the legal age.

Consent isn't a form you fill in once. It's part of the ongoing conversation between two people who care about each other's experience.

## Checking in, naturally

Checking in doesn't have to kill the mood. Simple, warm questions work well:

- "Is this okay?"
- "Do you like this?"
- "Do you want to slow down?"
- "What would feel good right now?"

Pay attention to body language too. If your partner goes quiet, tenses up, pulls away or seems unsure, pause and check. Stopping to ask shows respect and makes it safer for both of you to relax.

## Talking about sex outside the bedroom

Big conversations usually go better at a calm, neutral time, such as on a walk or over tea, rather than in the middle of intimacy or straight after something went wrong. Some tips:

- **Start positive:** "I really love it when we..."
- **Use "I" statements:** "I feel nervous when..." rather than "You always..."
- **Be curious:** ask open questions and listen to the answers without defending yourself.
- **Keep it short:** several small conversations over time are easier than one huge one.
- **Accept differences:** it's normal for partners to want different things or different amounts of intimacy. The aim is understanding and finding what works for both of you.

## Talking about your worries

If you're dealing with early ejaculation, erection difficulties or performance anxiety, telling your partner can feel risky. In practice, most partners respond with relief and support. They may have noticed something was wrong and worried it was about them. Being open usually lowers pressure, which can itself improve things. The lesson on talking about PE or ED has words you can use.

## Respecting "no" and "not now"

Hearing "not tonight" can sting, especially if you're already anxious. Try not to take it as rejection of you as a person. Thank your partner for being honest, stay affectionate, and avoid sulking or pressuring. A partner who knows they can say no safely is more likely to say yes freely.

## Consent and alcohol

Alcohol makes communication and consent harder. Someone who has had a lot to drink may not be able to make a clear, free choice, and your own judgement is affected too. If either of you has been drinking heavily, the safest and most respectful choice is to wait. Intimacy is better when you're both clear-headed and fully present.

## Nonverbal communication

Much of what partners communicate happens without words: a hand guiding yours, leaning in, a relaxed sigh, or pulling away. Learning to notice these signals, and checking in when you're unsure, builds trust. But never rely only on guessing. When in doubt, ask.

## When communication is hard

If conversations about intimacy often turn into arguments or silence, a couples counsellor or qualified sex therapist can help you both find better ways to talk. It's a sign of commitment to the relationship, not of failure.
