Once you can catch anxious thoughts, the next step is to answer them. This isn't about forcing yourself to think positively, which rarely works. It's about finding a thought that's more **balanced**: realistic, fair to you, and helpful.

## Questions to ask an anxious thought

Pick a thought from your journal and work through a few of these:

- **What's the evidence for it? What's the evidence against it?**
- **Is this a fact or a guess?**
- **Am I falling into a thinking trap?** Fortune telling, mind reading, all-or-nothing?
- **What would I say to a good friend who had this thought?**
- **What's the most likely outcome, not the worst?**
- **If the worst did happen, how would I cope?**
- **Will this matter in a week, a month, a year?**
- **Is this rule realistic, and where did I learn it?**

## Worked examples

**Thought:** "I'm going to lose my erection again tonight."
**Trap:** fortune telling, overgeneralising.
**Balanced thought:** "It happened once when I was exhausted and nervous. Lots of times it's been fine. If it happens, we can slow down and enjoy other things. It doesn't define me."

**Thought:** "She'll think I'm useless if I finish quickly."
**Trap:** mind reading.
**Balanced thought:** "I don't actually know what she thinks. She's been affectionate and patient. I can ask her what she enjoys, and there's more to sex than how long intercourse lasts."

**Thought:** "A real man should last for ages."
**Trap:** a "should" rule from porn and jokes.
**Balanced thought:** "Real-life averages are a few minutes. Many partners care more about closeness and attention than time. I'm working on control anyway, at my own pace."

## Make it believable

A balanced thought only helps if part of you believes it. If it feels fake, soften it. "I'm going to be amazing tonight" won't stick. "I don't know how tonight will go, and I can handle it either way" is more likely to.

In your journal, notice how the strength of the feeling changes from before to after. Even a drop from 8 to 6 is a real win.

## Behavioural experiments

Sometimes the best way to test a thought is to try something and see. If your thought is "If I tell my partner I'm nervous, they'll lose interest," a small experiment would be to share a little and notice what really happens. Most people find the result is kinder than predicted, and that becomes strong evidence against the anxious thought next time.

## A quick three-step version

When you don't have time for the full set of questions, try this short version in your head:

1. **Catch it:** "I'm having the thought that I'll fail."
2. **Check it:** "Is that a fact, or my anxiety guessing?"
3. **Change it:** "I don't know what will happen. I can slow down, breathe and stay close either way."

Adding the words "I'm having the thought that..." in front of an anxious thought is a simple trick that creates a little distance. It reminds you that you are the one noticing the thought, not the thought itself. Many people find that this alone lowers the intensity of the feeling.

## Practise when calm

Reframing is much easier when you're calm. Use your journal at quiet moments, after something has happened, or before an event you're worried about. Over time, the balanced thoughts start to come to mind more quickly in the moment, almost like a reflex.

## When reframing isn't enough

Some anxiety is rooted in past experiences, relationship difficulties or low mood, and thought work alone may not shift it. That's not a failure.
