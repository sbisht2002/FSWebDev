Sleep is one of the most powerful and most overlooked parts of sexual health. When you sleep well, your hormones, mood, energy and blood vessels all get a chance to recover. When you don't, desire, erections and patience can all suffer.

## Sleep and testosterone

Most of the day's testosterone is released during sleep, especially in the deeper stages. In a small but often-cited study, healthy young men who slept only five hours a night for one week had daytime testosterone levels 10 to 15 percent lower than when they were well rested. That's a drop similar to ageing by more than ten years. They also reported lower energy and wellbeing.

Testosterone isn't everything for erections, but it does influence desire and energy, and very low levels can contribute to erection problems.

## Sleep and erections

During healthy sleep, men naturally have several erections each night, mostly during dreaming (REM) sleep. These are thought to help keep the tissue in the penis healthy by refreshing it with oxygen-rich blood. Waking with an erection is a common sign that the blood vessels and nerves involved are working.

Poor sleep, especially from **sleep apnoea**, is linked with erection problems. Sleep apnoea means your breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep. Signs include loud snoring, gasping or choking at night, waking unrefreshed and feeling very sleepy during the day. It's common, especially in men who are overweight, and it's treatable. If this sounds like you, please see a doctor.

## Sleep, mood and anxiety

Short sleep makes stress hormones rise, mood dip and anxiety harder to manage, all of which can affect desire and performance. Tiredness also reduces patience and connection with a partner.

## How much sleep?

Most adults need **7 to 9 hours** a night. Regularly getting less than 6 is linked with many health problems. Quality matters too: waking up refreshed is a better sign than the number of hours alone.

## Habits that help

- **Keep a regular schedule.** Go to bed and get up at roughly the same time every day, including weekends.
- **Get morning light.** Ten to twenty minutes of daylight soon after waking helps set your body clock.
- **Cut caffeine after early afternoon.** Tea, coffee, colas and energy drinks can disrupt sleep for hours.
- **Limit alcohol.** It may help you fall asleep, but it breaks up deep and dream sleep later in the night.
- **Wind down.** Spend the last 30 to 60 minutes before bed doing something calm: reading, stretching, breathing, or a sleep wind-down session from the audio library.
- **Dim screens.** Bright screens late at night keep your brain alert. Use night mode and put the phone away when you get into bed.
- **Keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet.** A fan, an eye mask or earplugs can help, especially in shared or noisy homes.
- **Move during the day.** Regular exercise improves sleep, but intense workouts very late at night can make it harder to settle.
- **Don't lie awake for ages.** If you can't sleep after 20 minutes or so, get up, do something calm in dim light, and go back when sleepy.

## Shift work and busy lives

If you work night shifts or long hours, perfect sleep may not be possible. Protect what you can: keep a consistent routine on work days, use blackout curtains for daytime sleep, and take short naps if they help.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you snore loudly and feel very sleepy during the day, if you've had trouble sleeping for more than a few weeks, or if poor sleep comes with low mood. Insomnia and sleep apnoea are both very treatable, and treating them often improves energy, mood and sexual health.
