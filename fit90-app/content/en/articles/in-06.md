What happens after intimacy can matter as much as what happens during it. The few minutes afterwards are a time when many people feel especially open and connected, or, if they're rushed, especially vulnerable. Caring for each other in those moments is sometimes called "aftercare".

## What the research says

A study of couples found that people who spent more time on affection after sex, such as cuddling, kissing and talking, reported greater sexual satisfaction, and in turn greater relationship satisfaction, months later. Even a few extra minutes of closeness seemed to make a difference. Partners noticed and valued it.

## What aftercare can look like

There's no single right way. It depends on what you both enjoy. Ideas include:

- staying close and cuddling for a while rather than rolling over or reaching for your phone
- talking softly, sharing what you enjoyed, or simply lying together quietly
- offering a glass of water, a blanket or help getting comfortable
- laughing about anything awkward, kindly and together
- checking in: "How are you feeling?" "Was that good for you?"

## Why it matters

- **It builds trust and connection.** Your partner knows you care about them, not only about sex.
- **It makes the next time easier.** Feeling cared for helps both of you relax and look forward to intimacy.
- **It eases anxiety.** If things didn't go as planned, affection afterwards reassures both of you that the relationship is about much more than one moment.
- **It's a natural time to talk.** Gentle feedback and appreciation land well when you're both relaxed.

## If things didn't go as planned

If you finished sooner than you hoped, or your erection faded, aftercare is especially important. Rather than apologising repeatedly or withdrawing, stay close and affectionate. A simple "I'm really glad to be here with you" says more than an apology. If you'd like to talk about it, choose a calm moment later rather than right then, and keep the focus on the two of you as a team.

## Different needs

Partners may want different things afterwards. One might want to cuddle and talk; the other might want quiet or to sleep. Neither is wrong. Talk about it at a neutral time, ask what each of you likes, and look for a balance that works for both.

## Aftercare beyond the bedroom

Closeness doesn't only happen around intimacy. Everyday affection, such as a hug when you get home, holding hands, kind words and making time to talk, builds the connection that makes intimacy feel safe and enjoyable. Many couples find that taking care of this "everyday intimacy" improves their sex life more than any technique.

## Aftercare for yourself

Aftercare isn't only about your partner. If you tend to feel anxious, self-critical or low after intimacy, notice those feelings with kindness. A few slow breaths, a reassuring thought ("I'm learning, and that's okay"), and staying close rather than retreating into your own head can help. Being gentle with yourself makes it easier to be warm with your partner.

## When feelings come up

Intimacy sometimes brings up unexpected emotions, such as tearfulness, vulnerability or old memories. That's not unusual. Gentle reassurance and listening usually help. If strong feelings come up regularly, or are linked to past difficult experiences, a counsellor or therapist can offer support.

## A small habit to try

For the next few weeks, make a point of spending at least ten minutes together afterwards before getting up or picking up your phone. Notice how it feels for both of you. Then ask your partner what they'd like aftercare to look like. You may be surprised by how much small gestures mean.
