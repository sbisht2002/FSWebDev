Many men respond to sexual difficulties with harsh self-criticism: "I'm pathetic", "What's wrong with me?", "I've ruined it again." It can feel like being tough on yourself will push you to do better. In practice, harsh self-talk usually does the opposite. It ramps up anxiety and shame, and both of those make performance problems more likely.

Self-compassion is a different approach. It means treating yourself the way you'd treat a good friend who was struggling: honestly, but kindly.

## What self-compassion is (and isn't)

Researcher Kristin Neff describes three parts:

1. **Kindness instead of judgement:** speaking to yourself warmly, not harshly.
2. **Common humanity instead of isolation:** remembering that difficulties are part of being human, and that many men face exactly what you're facing.
3. **Mindfulness instead of over-identifying:** noticing painful feelings without letting them swallow you.

Self-compassion **isn't** making excuses, giving up or pretending everything's fine. You can be kind to yourself and still work hard on change. In fact, studies suggest self-compassion is linked with more motivation and resilience, not less, because you're less afraid of failing.

## Why it helps with sexual confidence

Shame tells you to hide and avoid. Self-compassion lets you face things. When you can say "That was disappointing, and it's okay; this happens to lots of people," you're more likely to talk to your partner, keep practising and seek help if you need it. You also take a lot of pressure off the next time, which by itself can improve how things go.

## A self-compassion break

When you notice you're beating yourself up, try this three-part pause, which takes under a minute:

1. **Acknowledge it:** "This is a hard moment." or "This really hurts."
2. **Remember you're not alone:** "Lots of men go through this. I'm not the only one."
3. **Offer kindness:** put a hand on your chest if it feels okay and say something like "May I be patient with myself," or simply, "I'm doing my best, and that's enough for today."

It may feel awkward at first. That's normal.

## The friend test

When a critical thought appears, ask: "What would I say to a close friend who told me this?" You'd probably be understanding and practical: "One bad night doesn't mean anything. You were exhausted. Be gentle with yourself." Then try saying the same thing to yourself.

## Rewriting the story

Take a recent experience that didn't go as you'd hoped. Write two short versions in your journal:

- **The harsh version:** what your inner critic says.
- **The compassionate version:** what a wise, kind friend would say about the same event.

Notice how each version makes you feel, and which one makes you more likely to try again.

## Small daily practice

Self-compassion grows with repetition, like a muscle. Once a day, perhaps at bedtime, notice one moment when you were hard on yourself and rewrite it in a kinder voice. It doesn't have to be about sex: a mistake at work or a missed workout counts too. The habit of answering your inner critic gently carries over into the moments that matter most.

## Compassion for your partner, too

Self-compassion often spreads. When you're less defensive about your own difficulties, it's easier to be patient and curious with your partner, and to talk openly about what you both want. Many couples find that the biggest improvements come not from better technique but from more warmth and less pressure.

## When shame runs deep

Sometimes harsh self-criticism has deep roots in past experiences, upbringing or depression.
